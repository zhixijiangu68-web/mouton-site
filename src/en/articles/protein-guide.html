How to choose protein powder: how much you need, and whey or soy

{{ readMinutes }} min read

Start strength training, and the question "should I take protein powder?" comes up right away.

Protein powder isn't medicine or a special food. It's protein in powdered form. So the first thing to think about isn't "which protein powder is best," but whether you're already getting enough from food.

With strength training, it works. But the effect is small

A meta-analysis of 49 randomized controlled trials (1,863 people) that added protein supplements to at least six weeks of strength training found that supplementing led to greater gains in strength and fat-free mass (the weight of muscle and everything other than fat) [1].

But the difference wasn't big. The difference in fat-free mass averaged about 0.3 kg, and in one-rep max on lifts like the bench press, about 2.5 kg. Better to think of protein powder as something that gives the effects of training a small boost, not a substitute for training.

How much do you need?

In the same meta-analysis, once total daily protein went beyond about 1.6 g per kg of body weight, more protein barely added any fat-free mass [1]. At 60 kg body weight, about 100 g a day is one guide to the upper limit.

Meanwhile, Japan's Dietary Reference Intakes (2025 edition) set the recommended protein intake for ages 18–64 at 65 g for men and 50 g for women [2]. That's an amount for staying healthy, not for building muscle.

If you regularly eat meat, fish, eggs, soy foods, and dairy, food often covers the recommended amount. The order to think in is: if you lift and food alone doesn't reach 1.6 g/kg, make up the shortfall with protein powder.

POINT

Protein powder isn't "drink it and you'll build muscle." It's "something to make up what your meals are missing."

Whey or soy?

Protein powders include milk-based whey and casein, and soy-based protein. A meta-analysis of nine studies comparing effects when combined with strength training found no difference in gains in strength or fat-free mass between soy and animal proteins such as whey [3].

  • If milk tends to upset your stomach, or you prefer plant-based → soy
  • If you choose by taste, how well it mixes, or price → go with what you like

Who should be careful

If you have kidney disease, or a doctor has told you to limit protein, don't add protein powder on your own judgment; talk to your doctor. The same goes if you're pregnant, or take medication for a chronic condition.

Summary

  • Combined with strength training, protein supplements make strength and muscle a little easier to gain (the effect is small)
  • Beyond about 1.6 g per kg of body weight, more protein barely added muscle
  • The recommended amount for health is 65 g for adult men and 50 g for women (Japan's Dietary Reference Intakes, 2025). First check whether your meals cover it
  • No difference in strength-training results was seen between whey and soy

As I wrote in "I cut back on strength training, and somehow got more stamina," building bigger muscles and having energy for the day aren't the same thing. Decide what you're taking it for, then choose.
