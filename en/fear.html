Maybe fear is discomfort, not danger

4 min read

Contents

  2. Rename "scared" as "not used to it yet"
  3. Nerves can also be read as excitement
  4. But don't ignore real danger
  5. Touch small discomforts every day
  6. Confidence comes after action
  7. Today's exercise
  8. References

When you feel afraid, first tell apart "dangerous" from "just uncomfortable." If it's only uncomfortable, you can move, a little at a time.

When we start something new, we often feel afraid. But is that fear really warning us of danger?

"What if I fail?" "What if people laugh?" "What if I lose money?" Feelings like these are a big reason we don't act.

But I want to stop there for a moment and think.

QUESTION

Is this actually dangerous?
Or is it just uncomfortable?

Fear and danger are not the same

The human brain is built to be wary of the unknown, the unpredictable, and anything that might fail. Long ago, that judgment could be a matter of life and death.

But most of the fear we feel today isn't life-threatening.

  • Starting a new business
  • Posting your thoughts on social media
  • Running an ad for the first time
  • Talking to a stranger

What we feel in moments like these is usually closer to discomfort than to danger itself.

Rename "scared" as "not used to it yet"

When we feel fear, we tend to take it as a sign to stop.

So try swapping the words.

  • "I'm scared" → "I'm not used to this yet"
  • "I'm scared" → "I'm nervous because it's unfamiliar"

Then fear stops looking like a stop sign and starts looking like a sign that you're stepping a little past your comfort zone.

It's like strength training, where muscles don't grow much without load. That's only a metaphor, of course.

Nerves can also be read as excitement

Right before a big game or presentation, your heart races and your palms sweat. You can read that as "I'm scared," or as "I'm excited."

In a study by Brooks (2014), people who reframed their anxiety as excitement did better on tasks such as giving a speech and solving math problems.

You may not be able to make the body's reaction disappear, but you can change what that reaction means.

Not "I'm nervous, so I'll quit," but "I'm nervous because I'm taking on something that matters."

But don't ignore real danger

It would be wrong to treat every fear as imagined.

  • Taking on large debt
  • Putting your living expenses into investments
  • Pushing so hard you damage your health
  • Ignoring laws or rules

Those are real risks, not discomfort.

What we need isn't to ignore fear, but to tell "danger" from "discomfort."

To tell them apart, write down the worst case concretely. "I lose a little money." "It doesn't sell and I start over." Put into plain words instead of feelings, it often turns out not to be "my life is over."

Touch small discomforts every day

You don't have to take a giant leap right away.

If speaking in front of people scares you, start with one person. Then three. Then ten.

You can practice in daily life too.

  • Wake up a little earlier than usual
  • Do the task you've been putting off first
  • Share your own opinion

Get used to it bit by bit like this, and what used to scare you becomes normal. Your comfort zone itself gets wider.

Confidence comes after action

Many people think, "I'll act once I feel confident." But the order is often the other way around.

Act → fail a little → learn you're still okay → act again.

Bandura (1977) placed experiences of your own accomplishment as a key source of self-efficacy, the sense that "I can do this."

This is where the idea of "doing what you said you'd do" comes in.

Every time you keep a small promise, like "I'll write an article today" or "I'll be in bed by 10 p.m.," you build a track record that says, "I do what I decide."

SAY / DO

Don't wait for confidence before you move.
The record of moving becomes confidence.

Once I'm ready. Once it's perfect. Wait like that and you'll never start.

Do the thing, get the power.

The strength comes after you act.

Today's exercise

From the things you're putting off, write down one that is "uncomfortable, but not dangerous."

Then do just the first five minutes of it today.

You don't need to get rid of fear.

What matters more is not making it your reason to stop.

If it's danger, protect yourself. If it's only discomfort, take a small step.

References

Brooks, A. W. (2014). Get Excited: Reappraising Pre-Performance Anxiety as Excitement. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143(3), 1144–1158.

Bandura, A. (1977). Self-efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change. Psychological Review, 84(2), 191–215.

Note: "Growth happens outside your comfort zone" is not a finding from any single study; in this article it is used as a metaphor.
