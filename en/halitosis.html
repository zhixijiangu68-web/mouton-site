Most bad breath comes from the mouth,
not the stomach

What data from 2,000 halitosis-clinic patients says about the tongue, gums, and saliva

Sometimes, talking with someone, the thought crosses my mind:

"Does my breath smell?"

According to a 2019 Japanese survey on bad breath, about 90% of people have worried about their own breath at some point. It's something almost everyone goes through.

And many people probably think:

Maybe my stomach's off. Maybe it's what I ate yesterday.

But look at the research, and the answer is much closer to home. Most bad breath comes from inside the mouth. At the center are tongue coating, gum disease, and dry mouth.

In this article, I use papers and public health information to sort out how bad breath works and what measures research supports.

The 30-second summary

• 80–90% of bad breath originates in the mouth. Japan's health ministry e-Health Net site cites 87%.

• In a study of 2,000 halitosis-clinic patients, 76% had a cause in the mouth, mostly tongue coating and gum disease.

• The main odor compounds are hydrogen sulfide and methyl mercaptan, made by bacteria.

• Clean your tongue "once on waking, gently." Overdoing it damages the tissue.

• It's hard to judge your own breath. 16% of clinic patients were diagnosed with pseudo-halitosis or halitophobia.

1. Bad breath is gas made by bacteria

There are many bacteria in the mouth. Some of them, especially anaerobic bacteria that prefer low-oxygen environments, break down proteins and amino acids in saliva, food debris, shed tissue, blood and so on. What's produced in the process is volatile sulfur compounds (VSCs).

It's much like body odor arising from "sweat and skin bacteria." Bad breath is gas released when bacteria in the mouth break down their raw materials.

For bad breath that starts in the mouth, the main players are mostly these two:

Key VSCs

Methyl mercaptan — a pungent, rotten smell. A big contributor to bad breath from the mouth.

Hydrogen sulfide — a rotten-egg smell. The next major component.

Dimethyl sulfide — an unpleasant, sweetish smell. Can stand out in bad breath that originates outside the mouth or from the blood.

In a study by Tangerman and Winkel, the strength of bad breath from the mouth correlated well with concentrations of methyl mercaptan and hydrogen sulfide, and in all six cases with causes outside the mouth, dimethyl sulfide was high.

At least in this study, the mix of VSCs gave a clue to whether bad breath came from inside or outside the mouth.

2. What 2,000 clinic patients showed about causes

One piece of data that concretely backs up "bad breath comes from the mouth" is an analysis of 2,000 consecutive patients who visited the halitosis clinic at KU Leuven in Belgium.

Causes in 2,000 halitosis-clinic patients

Cause in the mouth: 76%

• Tongue coating only: 43%

• Gingivitis/periodontitis only: 11%

• Both tongue coating and gum disease: 18%

Pseudo-halitosis / halitophobia: 16%

Ear, nose and throat, or other causes outside the mouth: 4%

Note: figures from the original abstract. The breakdown doesn't add up to 100% because some portion isn't given in the abstract.

Simply adding them up, tongue coating was involved in about 60% (43 + 18), and gum disease in about 30% (11 + 18). These two explain most of the people whose cause was in the mouth.

Another review puts causes at about 85% in the mouth, about 10% ear, nose and throat, and about 5% digestive or endocrine. A systematic review also gives oral factors as 80–90%, with tongue coating, periodontal disease, and poor oral hygiene as the main causes.

But the 2,000 were "people who visited a halitosis clinic," not a group representing the general population. The "80–90%" figure comes from reviews combining multiple studies, which is separate evidence from the 2,000-patient data (76%).

3. One of the biggest sources: the tongue

You brush every day, and your breath still worries you. What's easy to overlook then is the tongue.

When you look at your tongue in a mirror, there's sometimes a whitish layer on the surface. That's tongue coating. It's built up from food debris, shed surface cells, components of saliva, and bacteria.

Japan's e-Health Net explains that VSCs are produced most on the tongue. The back of the tongue in particular has a complex, uneven surface where bacteria easily cling and where saliva's rinsing doesn't reach well.

That tongue coating was involved in about 60% of the 2,000 patients fits this explanation.

4. Morning breath isn't necessarily a disease

Your mouth smells right after you wake up. Most people experience this, and it isn't abnormal.

During sleep, saliva production drops. Saliva rinses the mouth and curbs bacterial growth. When that weakens, bacteria become more active.

According to e-Health Net, tongue coating tends to increase on waking and when fasting, and the strength of bad breath fluctuates through the day in step with meals. This daily variation has been reported in classic studies too.

This "physiological bad breath" fades when you eat, drink, and clean your teeth and tongue. It isn't necessarily a disease.

5. Why a dry mouth smells more

Saliva is a factor you can't ignore when thinking about bad breath. Dry mouth and reduced saliva flow are named as factors related to bad breath in an expert consensus paper too.

Not talking for a long time, not drinking, being nervous, mouth breathing. When your mouth dries out in situations like these, its rinsing power weakens, and bacteria and their raw materials linger in the mouth.

For dealing with bad breath, you need to look not only at "what to deodorize with" but at "is my mouth dry?"

6. Don't overlook bad breath from gum disease

The other big cause is gum disease. In the 2,000-patient study, it was involved in about 30%.

According to e-Health Net, many of the bacteria that cause gum disease are anaerobic bacteria that make VSCs. And unlike cavities, gum disease progresses with almost no pain.

Your gums bleed. Your gums are receding. Food gets stuck between your teeth more easily. People mention your breath more than before. When you notice changes like these, covering it up with mints or mouthwash may not solve it.

It's worth getting checked for gum disease at a dental clinic.

7. Should you suspect your stomach first?

The idea that "bad breath comes from the stomach" is persistent. It's true that bad breath with causes outside the mouth exists.

Among the 2,000 patients, ear, nose and throat or other causes outside the mouth were 4%. Another review gives about 10% ear, nose and throat and about 5% digestive or endocrine. A systematic review puts factors outside the mouth at 10–20%, linked to systemic diseases.

Some research has reported a link between H. pylori infection and bad breath. A 2016 meta-analysis found higher odds of bad breath in H. pylori-positive people, and a lower risk of persistent bad breath after successful eradication.

But that doesn't prove that "the stomach directly makes bad breath"; it's research summarizing an association with infection. Nor does it mean "most people with bad breath have a stomach cause." The overall picture, that causes in the mouth are by far the most common, doesn't change.

To sum up: first check tongue coating, gum disease, cavities, plaque, and dry mouth. If no dental cause is found and bad breath continues, or you have nose, throat, or digestive symptoms, look for a cause with another specialist. That order makes sense.

8. Tongue cleaning: gently, once on waking

If tongue coating is the cause, you might think scrubbing hard and often is the answer. But that can backfire.

e-Health Net describes checking the coating in a mirror, placing the tongue cleaner lightly at the back, and pulling it forward. Never pressing too hard is what matters.

Once on waking is enough. More than that risks damaging the tissue, and it should be stopped if the tongue has cuts or sores.

Mechanical tongue cleaning has been shown to reduce VSCs in the mouth. A systematic review of studies adding tongue cleaning to toothbrushing also reported good effects on breath measures in every experiment.

But that review also stated that data on its effect on chronic bad breath are insufficient. Tongue cleaning is a useful tool, but it can't be said to solve bad breath on its own.

Tongue cleaner

For cleaning tongue coating. Watching in a mirror, place it lightly at the back and pull forward. Don't press too hard; once on waking. Don't use it if your tongue has cuts or sores.

9. Reduce the source rather than cover it up

Mints, gum, mouthwash, strongly flavored toothpaste. They do make you feel fresh, but covering with a scent and reducing the cause are different things.

Basic order of priority

1. Brush your teeth

2. Clean between your teeth with floss or interdental brushes

3. If there's a lot of tongue coating, clean your tongue gently

4. Don't let your mouth dry out

5. If you have gum disease or cavities, get them treated

Guidelines for choosing tongue cleaners, interdental brushes, and mouthwash are in how to choose tools for bad breath.

If you're unsure about daily care, talking to a dentist or dental hygienist is the shortcut.

10. It's hard to judge your own breath

Finally, the trickiest point.

It's hard to judge your own breath accurately with your own nose.

Of the 2,000 halitosis-clinic patients, 16% were diagnosed with pseudo-halitosis or halitophobia.

On the other hand, some people don't notice until someone points it out. That's why people with bad breath from gum disease can be unaware of it.

If it really worries you, rather than checking yourself over and over, having a dentist examine it objectively will put your mind at ease sooner.

Conclusion: look first at the tongue, gums, and saliva

80–90% of bad breath has its cause in the mouth.

Among 2,000 halitosis-clinic patients, most of those with causes in the mouth had tongue coating or gum disease involved.

Morning breath happens in healthy people too. There are causes in the stomach, nose and elsewhere, but they're a minority overall.

When your breath worries you, there's no need to suspect your stomach right away, or to cover it with a strong scent.

Look first at your tongue, your gums, and whether your mouth is dry.

Not "covering up" the smell, but taking care of the place where the smell is made. That seems like the place to start.
