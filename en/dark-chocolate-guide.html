How to choose dark chocolate: don't go by the cacao percentage alone

4 min read

In "Is dark chocolate really good for you?" we saw that what research actually tested was less chocolate itself than a component called cocoa flavanols.

QUIZTrue or false: an 86% cacao chocolate always contains more of cacao's beneficial compounds than a 70% one.Show answer

False. Not always.

The cacao percentage includes cacao fat (cocoa butter). In research, the amount of these compounds matched the non-fat cocoa solids more closely.

So when choosing dark chocolate in a store, should you just pick the highest number: 70%, 86%, 95%? Looking into it, there are several things the number alone doesn't tell you.

First, check that it's legally "chocolate"

In Japan, where I live, chocolate labeling is set by an industry fair competition code, and the product name on the back of the package says "chocolate," "semi-chocolate," "chocolate confectionery," and so on [1]. Other countries have their own legal definitions.

To be called "chocolate," a product needs at least a certain amount of cacao content. If cacao is what you're after, first check that the product is labeled as chocolate, not as a chocolate-flavored confection.

The cacao percentage includes fat

The "cacao" percentage is the total of cacao-derived ingredients such as cocoa mass, cocoa powder, and cocoa butter [1]. In other words, it includes cocoa butter, the fat of the cacao bean.

A study of chocolate and cocoa products sold in the United States found that flavanol content matched the amount of non-fat cocoa solids more closely than the cacao percentage [2]. Higher cacao percentages do tend to have more flavanols, but products with the same number can differ.

POINT

The cacao percentage is a useful guide. But it doesn't decide the flavanol content on its own.

Processing reduces flavanols

A study of cocoa powders found that the more a cocoa had been "alkalized" (Dutch-processed, to reduce acidity and darken the color), the fewer flavanols it had. Non-alkalized cocoa averaged about 35 mg per gram, while heavily alkalized cocoa had about 4 mg [3].

When choosing cocoa, pick pure, unsweetened cocoa powder, not a drink mix with sugar and milk added. Then, if the product description says whether it's alkalized, check that too.

Heavy metals vary by product

A study that tested 72 cacao products in the United States from 2014 to 2022 found that a certain share exceeded daily limits for lead or cadmium based on California's standards. Organic products didn't have less [4]. Similar results have been reported in testing by a consumer organization [5].

But these were tests of products sold in the US, and may not apply as-is to products on the shelves where you live. The practical measures are not eating a lot every day and not always sticking to the same product.
