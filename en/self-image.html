Self-image changes
long-term behavior

4 min read

Contents

  2. How it works
  3. Where it sits in psychology
  4. The effect shows up long-term, not short-term
  5. To change it, act first
  6. More than goals: what kind of person you act as
  7. Summary

Self-image doesn't change reality directly. What changes reality is your daily choices, and their effect shows up only over a long time.

What self-image is

It's your sense of "I'm this kind of person."

I can't stick with things. I'm not the type to stand in front of people. I'm not someone who writes.

These feel like facts, but most are just interpretations built from past experience.

A time you couldn't keep up exercise turns into the conclusion, "I'm someone who can't stick with things."

How it works

Self-image affects results indirectly, through behavior.

Self-perception → expectations → behavior → results → stronger self-perception

If you think "I won't stick with it anyway," you try less, and quit at the first small failure.

Then your experience doesn't grow, and the belief "see, I can't stick with things" gets stronger.

Where it sits in psychology

A close concept is self-efficacy: the sense that "I can carry out this action." It was proposed by Albert Bandura.

People with higher self-efficacy tend to take on harder tasks, keep going after failure, and try again.

But it doesn't guarantee success. Ability, experience, environment, and luck matter a lot too.

Self-image isn't ability itself. It's one of the factors that decides whether you use your ability.

The effect shows up long-term, not short-term

Thinking "I can do it" today won't change tomorrow's results.

The difference comes from the number of attempts.

You think, "That's not for me." So you try less often.

Try less, and your experience doesn't grow, and your ability is slow to develop.

The problem isn't that a negative belief directly causes failure.

It's that it cuts the number of attempts success requires.

Over one, three, ten years, the gap in experience grows.

On the other hand, just chanting "I can" isn't enough either.

Grades don't go up without studying, and work doesn't get finished without making it.

To change it, act first

You don't need to force yourself to believe something to change how you see yourself.

Act in a small way and create evidence.

If you want to be someone who exercises, walk for ten minutes.

If you want to be someone who reads, read five pages.

If you want to be someone who makes things, finish one thing, however small.

Bandura emphasized mastery experiences, your own successes, as one of the strongest sources of self-efficacy.

There's also the idea that people form their self-perception by observing their own behavior: Bem's self-perception theory.

Act small → evidence that "I did it" remains → self-perception shifts a little → the next action gets easier

Keep that cycle going long enough, and "I happened to do it" becomes "I've been doing it a lot lately," and then "this is the kind of person I am."

More than goals: what kind of person you act as

A goal ends once you reach it.

A self-perception like "I'm someone who keeps learning" or "I'm someone who makes things" keeps steering your behavior even after that.

Decide on goals, and also on what kind of person you'll act as. It's easier to keep going that way.

Summary

The path by which self-image has an effect is a long one:

Self-image → small daily choices → repeated behavior → amount of experience → ability and results

In the short term, the effect is small.

But in the long run, it becomes a difference you can't ignore.

One last question.

The things you think "I can't do": are they really beyond your ability?

Or have you just been trying less often because you think "I'm not that kind of person"?

The most realistic way to change your self-image isn't to talk yourself into it.

It's to do something a little different from before, and keep doing it for a long time.
