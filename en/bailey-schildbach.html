Bailey Schildbach and the art of continuing

5 min read

Contents

  4. Continuing is a skill, not a mood
  5. What I want to borrow

She lives with pain, and she keeps training to run farther. The word she keeps coming back to isn't "winning" or "beating it." It's "continuing."

Some people talk about discipline as if it were a personality. You either have it or you don't.

Bailey Schildbach talks about it differently. On her channel, she describes herself as someone who speaks honestly about pain, discipline, and what it really takes to keep going. Not "what it takes to win." What it takes to keep going.

What kept me watching was simple: she doesn't pretend the hard part is over.

Who she is, in her own words

Everything in this section comes from how she describes herself on her own channel, podcast and website. I'm not adding anything she hasn't said publicly.

  • A runner, training for longer and longer distances
  • A writer, with a newsletter she calls a mix of an advice column and a personal journal
  • A former ballerina
  • A student, with a long road of study ahead of her
  • Someone living with chronic conditions that bring both physical and emotional pain

She has also written about an injury to her spine that, for a while, took away her ability to walk. Later, in her newsletter, she describes running 6.5 miles, something she says would have made her twelve-year-old self proud.

That's the part that got me. Not a marathon time. Not a podium. A distance that mattered because of where she started from.

Three titles that stopped me

She also hosts a podcast called Limitless. A few of her titles, across the podcast and her videos, read almost like a philosophy on their own:

PODCASTThe Art of Continuing (How to Live With Pain)
PODCASTWhy Struggle Is a Gift (and How to See It Differently)
VIDEOIf you're being realistic, you're already losing

I want to be honest about what follows. These are my thoughts, prompted by her titles and the way she presents her work. They are not a summary of everything she says. If you want her actual words, go to her channel. They're better than my paraphrase.

"Realistic" can be a way of quitting early

"If you're being realistic, you're already losing." The first time I read that, I disagreed. Isn't being realistic a good thing?

Then I thought about how I actually use the word. When I say "let's be realistic," I'm rarely looking at facts. I'm usually looking for permission. Permission to aim a little lower, so that I can't be disappointed.

For someone living with pain, "realistic" is a very available excuse. Nobody would blame her for stopping. And that's exactly why the title lands. It isn't telling you to ignore reality. It's asking whether "realistic" is a description of the world, or a decision you already made about yourself.

QUESTION

When I say "be realistic," am I describing the world, or protecting myself from it?

Continuing is a skill, not a mood

"The art of continuing" is the phrase I keep thinking about.

We usually treat continuing as something that happens when we feel strong enough. Motivation shows up, so we keep going. Motivation leaves, so we stop. In that model, continuing is a mood.

Calling it an art changes that. An art is something you practice. You get better at it. You learn the small moves: how to start on a bad day, how to do a smaller version instead of nothing, how to come back after a week you missed.

It reminds me of something I wrote about Camus and Sisyphus. Camus asked us to imagine Sisyphus happy. Not because the boulder got lighter, but because the meaning was in how he carried it. Bailey's version feels more practical: the boulder is real, and here is how you keep carrying it today.

It also connects to the Stoic idea I keep returning to in the article on Stoicism: some things are up to us, and some are not. The pain isn't up to her. Whether she laces up her shoes today, at least partly, is.

What I want to borrow

When I wrote about David Goggins, the thing I wanted to borrow was his "small strangeness": doing a little more than common sense says.

From Bailey, I want to borrow something quieter.

  • Measure from where you started, not from where others are. 6.5 miles is not impressive on paper. It's enormous if you once couldn't walk.
  • Don't wait for the pain to be gone. If the plan only works on good days, it isn't a plan. It's a hope.
  • Be suspicious of "realistic." Sometimes it's wisdom. Sometimes it's fear wearing a sensible coat.

None of this means pushing through everything. Pain is information, and listening to your body and to the people treating you is part of the art too. Continuing isn't the same as ignoring.

Where to find her

If any of this resonated, go to the source. Her YouTube channel, her podcast Limitless, and her newsletter are where she says it in her own voice.

Most of us aren't fighting what she's fighting. But all of us have a boulder of some kind.

The question isn't whether it's heavy. It's whether you'll practice carrying it.
